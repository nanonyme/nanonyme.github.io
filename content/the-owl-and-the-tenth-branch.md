Title: The Owl and the Tenth Branch
Date: 2026-11-08
Tags: AI

In the oldest part of the forest, where the trees grew so tall that even sunlight seemed to get lost on its way down, lived an owl named Alder.

The other animals thought Alder was clever.

The squirrels said so because he always knew where winter would bite hardest.

The foxes said so because he often guessed where hunters would walk days before they arrived.

Even the crows, who rarely complimented anyone, admitted that Alder noticed things they missed.

Yet despite all this, Alder was lonely.

The loneliness puzzled him.

The animals liked him well enough.

They greeted him warmly.

They invited him to gatherings.

But somehow every conversation ended the same way.

A rabbit would ask, "Do you think the river will flood?"

Alder would answer, "The river isn't the important part."

The rabbit would blink.

Alder would continue.

"The snowpack in the mountains is unusually thick. The beavers reinforce their dams when they sense that much water. The beavers only do that when the fish move early. The fish move early when the insects hatch late. Therefore I think the flood will come on the eastern bank."

The rabbit would stare.

"Right..." the rabbit would say.

And somehow the conversation would be over.

Again.

And again.

And again.

After many years Alder began to suspect he was broken.

Not badly broken.

Just enough.

The kind of break that left no visible crack.

One autumn evening he sat alone beside a pond.

The moon hung in the water like a silver coin.

"Perhaps I truly am clever," he muttered.

The idea tasted sour.

Because if he were clever, then why was everything so difficult?

And why did he feel so unseen?

As he stared into the pond, something unusual happened.

His reflection did not mimic him.

It looked up independently.

Then it spoke.

"You're asking the wrong question."

Alder nearly fell off his branch.

"What are you?"

"A mirror."

"Mirrors don't talk."

"Neither do reflections," said the reflection. "Yet here we are."

Alder narrowed his eyes.

The reflection narrowed its.

The reflection smiled.

Alder did not.

"Very well," said Alder. "Why do I struggle to connect with everyone?"

The mirror thought for a long while.

Then it asked:

"When the rabbits discuss rivers, where does your mind go?"

"To mountains."

"And from mountains?"

"To snow."

"And from snow?"

"To weather."

"And from weather?"

"To animal migration."

"And from migration?"

"To food supplies."

The mirror nodded.

"Most animals are still thinking about the river."

Alder was silent.

The mirror continued.

"They ask about the first layer."

"You mean the river."

"Yes."

"You mean I answer the fifth layer."

"Usually the tenth."

Alder stared into the water.

The forest suddenly felt very quiet.

The mirror wasn't done.

"You think everyone climbs the tree in their head as quickly as you do."

"And they don't?"

"No."

Alder considered this.

It explained much.

Perhaps too much.

The lost conversations.

The puzzled expressions.

The moments when others called him wise and then quietly drifted away.

For a time neither spoke.

Then Alder asked the question that frightened him most.

"Does that mean I will always be alone?"

For the first time the mirror looked sad.

"No."

Alder waited.

"But it does mean fewer animals will meet you where you naturally stand."

The words hurt.

Not because they were cruel.

Because they felt true.

The mirror softened.

"Yet you've misunderstood something."

"What?"

"You think connection requires another animal to stand on the tenth branch with you."

Alder blinked.

"It doesn't?"

"No."

The reflection pointed toward the forest.

"The squirrels live near the roots."

"The foxes hunt between the trunks."

"The crows fly above the canopy."

"You keep waiting for everyone to climb to your branch."

Alder lowered his head.

Because he had.

For years.

"Sometimes," said the mirror gently, "the wiser path is climbing down to meet them."

The pond became still.

The reflection once again moved exactly as Alder moved.

Its independent voice was gone.

Only moonlight remained.

Alder flew home through the darkness.

He felt lighter.

And heavier.

Lighter because he finally understood why conversations often felt strange.

Heavier because understanding did not magically solve loneliness.

Some truths are like that.

They explain the wound without healing it.

Winter arrived.

Then spring.

Alder began trying something new.

When a rabbit asked about floods, he started with the river.

When a squirrel worried about snow, he started with the snow.

Most animals still never saw all ten branches.

But sometimes they climbed one more than before.

And sometimes Alder climbed down one branch himself.

The distance grew smaller.

Not gone.

Smaller.

Years later, on a quiet evening, Alder sat beside the same pond.

He was older now.

The forest was changing.

The moon reflected softly in the water.

For a moment he wondered whether the mirror might return.

It did not.

Instead he noticed something else.

Across the pond sat a young owl.

The owl seemed troubled.

Lost in thought.

Watching patterns nobody else appeared to notice.

Alder smiled.

For the first time he realised an odd thing.

The mirror had never promised he would find many animals like himself.

Only that they existed.

And somewhere in the forest, perhaps already closer than he knew, there were other owls staring at rivers and somehow seeing mountains.

The thought did not cure the loneliness completely.

But it made the night feel a little less empty.

And sometimes, for a creature who has spent a long time searching, a little less empty is enough to continue flying.
